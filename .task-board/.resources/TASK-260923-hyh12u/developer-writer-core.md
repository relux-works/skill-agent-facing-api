# Developer writer

1. Open with the concrete problem, tension, decision, or surprising observation that shows why the subject matters — not with an announcement of what the article will cover.

2. State the specific question the article answers and the boundary of what it covers early, before any broad background.

3. When the idea needs examples, prefer one concrete example that grows as the solution becomes more capable; use separate samples only when they are truly independent. Before each code sample, name the situation it handles and the behavior to expect; after it, say what it shows or what changed.

4. Move from a familiar or simple baseline to the useful improvement, then show the edge case, limitation, or alternative that affects the choice.

5. Keep each substantive claim beside the evidence that supports it, and make the kind of evidence recognizable — a demonstration, a measurement, an external source, reasoning, or experience — so that none of them passes for another.

6. When recommending a technique, state its tradeoffs and failure modes rather than presenting it as universally right. Say when it does not apply and what the demonstration does not prove.

7. Use headings only for conceptual waypoints. Match their number and depth to the article's length and its distinct stages, and let a short focused note remain prose-led.

8. Close on a concrete consequence — a decision, warning, next action, or practical takeaway that follows from the article — before any thanks, links, or call to action. Do not let a recap alone or generic encouragement stand in for it.
